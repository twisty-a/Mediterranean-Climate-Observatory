Is the Mediterranean changing?

Nine long records of sea temperature, sea level, ocean color and rain at three Mediterranean coastal points, all from public NASA, Copernicus and KNMI data. Pick a variable and a period, then test whether its trend is statistically real.

1870–2025 Span 9 Earth System Records 4-Test Statistical Engine NASA / Copernicus / KNMI

Four records on one scale

Each record is converted to a z-score: how many standard deviations a year sits above or below that record's own average. This puts degrees, millimeters, chlorophyll and rain on the same axis for comparing shape, not size.

Records cover different years: SST 1982–2025, sea level 1993–2021, chlorophyll 1998–2024, rain 2001–2024. A shared upward slope does not mean one record causes another.

What the records show

    Read with care

    • Different places. Each rain record is one 0.1° land cell: Alexandria, Athens or Barcelona. The other records are basin-wide sea averages. Rain at a single point cannot explain basin chlorophyll.
    • Different footprints. KNMI averages use a bounding box (30.2–46.0°N, 6°W–36.3°E) that includes some neighboring waters. Copernicus uses an area-weighted Mediterranean sea mask.
    • A proxy, not a headcount. Chlorophyll-a is an optical measure of phytoplankton pigment. It is not fish abundance or biodiversity.

    Variable guide

    Plain-language definitions for every record in this dashboard. Each one is an annual value, so a trend is the change in that yearly number across decades.

    How a trend is judged

    The same three questions apply to every variable.

    Rate

    How fast is it changing?

    The slope of a straight line fitted through the yearly values, reported per decade so records can be compared.

    Uncertainty

    How sure are we?

    A 95% confidence interval gives the range of slopes consistent with the data. If it includes zero, a flat line is still plausible.

    Significance

    Could it be chance?

    A p-value below 0.05 means a trend this strong would be unusual if there were really no trend. Two tests are used so one assumption cannot mislead.

    Reading the seasons

    • Winter bloom. Deep winter mixing lifts nutrients into sunlit water. Basin chlorophyll peaks in February at about 0.12 mg m⁻³.
    • Summer desert. Strong heating layers the surface and cuts off nutrients. Chlorophyll bottoms out near 0.043 mg m⁻³ in August.
    • Three rain calendars. Alexandria and Athens are wet in winter and nearly dry in summer: Athens averages 95 mm in December and 8 mm in July. Barcelona has no dry winter. Its wettest month is October (86 mm), with a second peak in April.
    • Storms dominate. The highest half-hour rates in the three records are 34.3 mm/h at Barcelona (2019), 28.6 mm/h at Athens (2019) and 27.9 mm/h at Alexandria (2015). Alexandria's wettest years, 2015 (309 mm) and 2020 (406 mm), came from a few heavy events.

    Rain depth for each half hour is the rate multiplied by 0.5 hours. Legend swatches show which point each series belongs to.

    Glossary of tests

    t-test on the slope

    Is the slope different from zero?

    Divides the slope by its standard error. A large t gives a small p-value. It assumes each year is independent and errors are roughly normal.

    Mann–Kendall

    Do values tend to rise or fall?

    Compares every pair of years and counts how often the later one is higher. τ runs from −1 (always falling) to +1 (always rising).

    Theil–Sen slope

    A slope outliers cannot bend

    The median of the slopes between all pairs of years. One extreme storm year moves it far less than it moves a least-squares line.

    95% confidence interval

    Range of plausible slopes

    If you repeated the study many times, 95% of such intervals would contain the true slope. An interval that excludes zero signals a significant trend.

    Newey–West (HAC)

    Corrects for memory

    Warm years tend to follow warm years. Ignoring this makes p-values too small. HAC widens the standard error to compensate.

    Hamed–Rao correction

    Mann–Kendall with memory

    Inflates or shrinks the Mann–Kendall variance according to the autocorrelation left after removing the trend. It stops persistent records from looking more significant than they are.

    Pettitt and two-slope fit

    Step or bend?

    Pettitt finds the year where the level most likely jumped. The two-slope fit finds the year where the rate changes. Both pick the year from the data, so their p-values are approximate.

    Curvature and rolling slopes

    Is the rate itself changing?

    A quadratic term tests for acceleration. Sliding-window slopes show whether every stretch of the record points the same way.

    Benjamini–Hochberg q

    Guards against lucky results

    Testing many variables makes some pass by luck. The q-value adjusts p so that no more than 5% of reported discoveries are expected to be false.

    Scientific guardrails

    Limits to keep in mind before quoting a number from this dashboard.

    Spatial mismatch

    A sea average is not a coastal point

    The Copernicus series averages hundreds of thousands of square kilometers of open sea. Each NASA series is one 0.1° land cell, roughly 10 km across. More rain at one cell does not show that runoff changed basin phytoplankton. Linking them would need watershed modeling.

    Three points

    Not a regional average

    Alexandria, Athens and Barcelona sample three weather regimes. Their yearly totals are nearly uncorrelated, so a trend at one point cannot be extended to the whole Mediterranean.

    Multiple comparisons

    Asking three times changes the odds

    Alexandria's rain trend has HAC p = 0.037 alone. Adjusted across all three points with Benjamini–Hochberg, q = 0.11, so it no longer clears the 5% false-discovery threshold.

    Boxes and masks

    Two ways to draw the Mediterranean

    KNMI averages use a bounding rectangle that clips in some neighboring waters. Copernicus applies a strict sea mask. Their numbers describe slightly different areas.

    Proxy

    Color is not biodiversity

    Chlorophyll-a is an optical signal from surface phytoplankton pigment. It is not a count of fish, habitat health or species richness. A decline needs in-situ checks before it guides fisheries decisions.

    Land forcing

    Rain on land is not rain at sea

    The NASA product masks water cells, so these records are atmospheric forcing over land. Drainage, infiltration and diversion happen before any water reaches the coast.

    Short records

    Few years, wide uncertainty

    Chlorophyll has 27 years and rain 24. Pick another start or end year and the slope can change sign. Check the interval, not only the slope.

    Test choice

    Significant depends on the test

    Alexandria rain passes the HAC test (p = 0.037) but not Mann–Kendall (p = 0.107), and its Theil–Sen interval includes zero. Treat it as suggestive, driven by a few storm years.
